# Model 15: cooperation, punishment, conformity, and morality

## Claims from the debate

The discussion makes two distinct claims.

First, an evolutionary/cultural account is offered for moral behavior: individuals
who are completely rigid or completely norm-breaking may fare poorly, while many
people track the social norm; cooperation, punishment, and group membership can
therefore be shaped by survival and social selection.

Second, an objection is raised: **group acceptance cannot define morality**,
because groups can demand behavior that we independently judge immoral. If
conformity sometimes promotes wrongdoing, conformity cannot by itself supply the
meaning or truth of moral propositions.

## What the model tests

Model 15 combines two descriptive forces:

1. **payoff pressure**: cooperation has a cost, while defectors can be punished;
2. **conformist transmission**: whichever behavior is already common gets an
   additional copying advantage.

The pedagogical selection gradient is

```math
g(x)=p x-c+k(2x-1),
```

where:

- `x` is the cooperative fraction;
- `c` is cooperation cost;
- `p` is punishment strength against defectors;
- `k` is conformity strength.

The dynamic is

```math
\dot x = x(1-x)g(x).
```

This is not a fitted model of one historical society. It is a transparent
mechanism model.

## Cooperation

Without reciprocity, punishment, assortment, or another mechanism, costly
cooperation can decline.

Sufficient punishment can reverse the payoff gradient and stabilize high
cooperation. This is consistent with experimental public-goods work showing that
cooperation flourishes when costly punishment of defectors is available and
breaks down when punishment is excluded.

Evolutionary theory provides several additional mechanisms for cooperation,
including kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network
reciprocity, and group selection.

## Conformity

Conformity creates positive frequency dependence.

If a norm begins above 50%, conformist transmission pushes it upward. If it
begins below 50%, conformity pushes it downward.

Crucially, the conformity equation contains **no moral label**. If the majority
behavior is cooperative, conformity can stabilize cooperation. If the majority
behavior is harmful, discriminatory, violent, or dishonest, the same
content-neutral mechanism can stabilize that behavior.

This matches the conceptual objection raised in the debate.

## Punishment plus conformity

Boyd and Richerson's cultural-evolutionary work shows that even weak conformist
transmission can stabilize costly norm enforcement under specified conditions.
This helps explain why communities can maintain cooperation despite individual
temptations to free ride.

But the same general machinery can support local norm persistence without
determining whether the norm is morally justified.

## Judgment on the claims

### Descriptive evolutionary claim

**Supported in a qualified sense.** Evolutionary and cultural mechanisms can
generate and stabilize cooperation, norm following, punishment of defectors, and
conditional adaptation to group behavior. The debate is scientifically justified
in treating these as phenomena that can be modeled without assuming a
supernatural moral module.

The model does not support a literal universal claim that "90% of people" occupy
one conformist psychological category. That numerical distribution would require
empirical measurement and an operational definition.

### Normative reduction claim

**Rejected as a derivation of moral truth.** The model directly demonstrates why:
conformity is content-neutral. It can stabilize a beneficial norm or a harmful
norm using exactly the same dynamics.

Therefore an evolutionary explanation of why humans acquire moral intuitions or
why groups enforce norms does **not** by itself establish that the resulting
norms are objectively right.

This is an is/ought boundary, not a failure of evolutionary biology.

### What would be needed for the stronger moral claim

To move from descriptive moral evolution to objective moral truth, an argument
needs an additional normative premise or metaethical theory. No population
dynamic can derive that premise from reproductive success, conformity, or
stability alone.
